### 1.2 Placeholder-preserving decimal reversal

For a positive integer $n=10^k m$, where $m$ is not divisible by ten, define:

$$I(n)=10^k\operatorname{rev}(m).$$

The trailing-zero placeholders remain at the same magnitude. Define $I(0)=0$ for completeness.

The following examples all use this primary register.

| Original span | Core reversal | Inverse span |
|---:|---|---:|
| 120 | 12 → 21 | 210 |
| 390 | 39 → 93 | 930 |
| 400 | 4 → 4 | 400 |
| 2000 | 2 → 2 | 2000 |
| 2700 | 27 → 72 | 7200 |
| 3970 | 397 → 793 | 7930 |
| 12600 | 126 → 621 | 62100 |

This operation is digit reversal, not the reciprocal $1/n$. In this register, $I(I(n))=n$. A self-inverse span returns its source date when rebuilt from the same anchor.

Digit reversal preserves the digit sum, so the difference between a span and its inverse is divisible by 9. That elementary decimal property does not imply preservation of divisibility by 23.

The older segment-level Rounded inverse axiom remains a separate dependency. For example, a segment of 365 reverses to 563 and is normalized to 565 when that chain procedure is explicitly opened. The matched-anchor whole spans in this survey are multiples of ten and need no such normalization.

## 9. Probability benchmark and its scope

### 9.1 Why the working density is 1/23

Among successive multiples of five, one in every 23 is divisible by 115. Equivalently, division by five turns the question into divisibility by 23. This supplies the simple working density:

$$p=\frac{1}{23}.$$

The last-digit split does not supply an additional factor of two. For a 6-ending inverse date measured to 6 BC, the span is divisible by ten and a successful quotient in units of 115 is even. For a 1-ending inverse date, the span ends in 5 and the successful quotient is odd. Each class has one qualifying position per 23 successive ten-year positions.

This is a density statement on complete residue cycles. It is not a demonstration that the historically related source dates, or the inverse outputs of this finite selected sample, are uniformly distributed among those residues.

### 9.2 Original benchmark: the nine-hit excess

$$E[X]=\frac{64}{23}\approx2.7826.$$

That is approximately 0.175%, or one in 571. The discussion's shorthand of about one in 500 is a reasonable rounding of this unadjusted model result. The stated probability is for nine or more hits, not exactly nine.
