The Birth and Cross of Christ in Symbolic Time

A companion study connecting Books 1 and 2

Day–Year Studies • The 360 calendar and the Key of 23

“For Christ also suffered for sins once for all, the righteous for the unrighteous, to bring you to God.”

1 Peter 3:18, BSB

Book 1 follows the different calendar witnesses toward the Cross. Book 2 turns back toward Christ’s birth and then follows the heavenly testimony across the larger history of Israel and the nations. Between those inquiries lies a numerical witness that needs room of its own. The days surrounding Christ’s birth and Passion can be read as symbolic years and compared with the chronology of Creation, the Flood, the Exodus, and the Conquest. The same calendar framework that gathers the Gospel dates also supplies the measures for these longer comparisons.

I find a consistent direction in them. At the birth, the comparison gathers the positions associated with Noah’s three sons. At the Passion, a span expressed as 666 multiples of 23 expands into a doubled 7770. Its increase of 222 units leads, when read back as days, to the reconstructed lunar Day of Atonement in AD 33. Further operations carry the Creation comparison backward to 7006 BC, exactly 7000 years before the 6 BC Christ datum. The calculations give definite forms to the possibility considered in Book 1: the Cross may be the center from which the renewal of time proceeds.

I retain AD 30 beside AD 33 throughout this inquiry. Both belong to the Friday Passion possibilities considered in Book 1, and AD 30 also marks the spring beginning of the final three years leading to AD 33. The day–year comparisons give that pairing a further expression: the two years receive related spans, expansions, and Creation patterns. The two ministry beginnings also frame a three–four–three arrangement whose repeated autumn date returns us to Noah’s departure from the ark.

My purpose is to follow that possibility carefully. An exact calculation establishes a relationship between stated dates. A historical reconstruction proposes how an ancient calendar may have been observed. A theological reading asks what the relationship may signify. I want the reader to be able to follow each step without losing the difference between them. The argument does not require every person in the narratives to have known the numerical pattern. It asks whether the One who entered their history may have ordered more than its human participants could see.

The main study develops the argument in sequence, with the Masoretic Text (MT) supplying its principal chronology. The appendices retain the fuller Gear tables, comparisons with the Samaritan Pentateuch (SP) and Septuagint (LXX), seasonal qualifications, and the secondary calculations that would otherwise interrupt the reading. Book 1’s completed chapters and the Unified Chronology’s current Markdown files remain the controlling references for their respective subjects.1

1 The calendar and the symbolic year

The day–year sign has a biblical setting in Ezekiel 4:4–6. Ezekiel bears the years of the people’s iniquity through a measured number of days. The present study extends that mode of comparison to a declared calendar frame. A literal day occupies a numbered position; that position is then displayed as a symbolic year. The resulting date is a coordinate for comparison. Symbolic AD 13873, for example, represents a day in literal AD 33. It is not a prediction about a future civil year.

The selected Christ frame begins at Nisan 1 in 6 BC, corresponding to Julian February 21, JD 1719282.5. I designate that opening boundary Day 0. A day receives its numbered position through completion: the first day ends at count 1, and the thirteenth day ends at count 13. That completed count supplies the symbolic AD label. The Julian-day tables identify the opening midnight of each corresponding day, so their timestamp differences must retain that earlier boundary. The companion Exodus frame begins thirty days later, on Julian March 23, 6 BC. This study uses the Christ frame throughout, unless another convention is expressly named.2

There are two distinct birth-calendar settings. Julian December 25, 1 BC, occupies symbolic AD 2135. Gregorian December 25, 1 BC, occurs two literal days later and occupies symbolic AD 2137. These are two same-named calendar dates, not two labels for one instant. Each calendar then carries its own seven elapsed days from December 25 to January 1, the eighth day counted inclusively.
