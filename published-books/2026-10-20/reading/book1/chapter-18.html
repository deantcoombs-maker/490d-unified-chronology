The earthly events and the heavenly view

“For what is seen is temporary, but what is unseen is eternal.”

— 2 Corinthians 4:18

This book began with the Passover dates. Its purpose has always been larger: to see Christ's Passion as an act in which God brings together time, peoples, and traditions that look divided from where we stand. I have tried to follow John far enough above our usual position to see the events of earth within their heavenly setting.

I take that setting seriously. Heaven is real. What happens there matters as much as what happens here, and the unseen order endures beyond what we can presently observe. Earthly history matters because God entered it. But the account of what people saw on earth does not exhaust what God was doing.1

That is the perspective I want to carry out of Book 1 and into Book 2. We have examined the days of the Cross. Next we will return to Christ's birth and then follow the celestial witness across the larger history, from Abraham through the fall of Jerusalem and the spread of the Gospel around AD 70. The subject continues. We are changing where we look.

One Passion seen through different calendars

The difficulty was the relation between the Passover meal and Christ's death. The Synoptics present Jesus eating the Passover before His death; John presents the authorities still anticipating it as Jesus is brought before Pilate. I have read these accounts through two month beginnings, giving the same Crucifixion Friday the names Nisan 15 and Nisan 14.2 In that proposed harmony, the One who gives the meal also gives Himself. The different reckonings bring us to the same Passion.

The calendar work was necessary because a day is always being named from somewhere. Sunset, midnight, and dawn place their boundaries differently. Earlier and later month beginnings give different numbers to the same civil date. Those differences have to remain visible if the proposed joining is to mean anything. Calling everything the same would remove the very witnesses I am trying to bring together.

The historical questions also remain where we left them. A diagram cannot tell us which official accepted a particular crescent report or how every community arranged its sacrifices. It can show what follows from the boundaries we have specified. I have tried to give the historical question its proper place while asking what God might accomplish through the different reckonings.

We have kept Pharisees and Sadducees in view, along with Galileans, Judeans, Essenes, and Samaritans. Egyptian and Roman reckonings have their place as the field widens. These are peoples with histories, disputes, and ways of ordering their days. Their inclusion matters to the purpose of the book. I am asking how God can gather their witness around Christ without waiting for them to settle every disagreement first.3

That is a stronger claim than finding a convenient way to reconcile two dates. It concerns God's sovereignty over the people, their traditions, and the time they inhabit. Their divisions do not leave Him at their mercy. He can act within the world they actually occupy and give their different reckonings a place in one fulfillment.

The Resurrection makes the limit of our position especially clear. Mary reaches the tomb while it is dark and finds the stone already removed. The act has taken place before she understands it. Her understanding follows, and John lets us watch it follow. The victory does not begin when its human witnesses work out what has happened.4

Our calendar comparison belongs within that same limit. It locates the accounts through their different beginnings; it does not recover the instant at which Christ rose. The ordinary dawn-start day and the phased morning convention in our chart retain their own names and rules. They can contribute to the testimony without being forced into a universal pre-dawn agreement.

The Lord whom Mary encounters is greater than the portion of the event she first understands. That is also true of us as readers. We should expect to learn from the particulars while remaining aware that our position does not give us the whole view.

What I mean by the center of time

I have treated the Cross as a possible source of the order we have been tracing. I mean that seriously. The suggestion is that its actual accomplishment may reach backward into the past as well as forward into the future. Earlier events may receive their form from the fulfillment that, from our position in history, has not yet happened.

I do not know how God would do this. I am willing to consider that He does. The Word through whom creation came into being entered the days of that creation. Paul brings creation, its continuing existence in Christ, and reconciliation through the blood of His Cross into one account. That gives me reason to ask a larger question about time than the placing of one event after another.5

The centrality of the Cross therefore does not require a calculated first day and last day with Calvary halfway between them. Particular frames can have an exact midpoint. The larger claim concerns the One who gives those days their existence and their purpose. The Cross stands within the history it may also be ordering.

Noon has been important to that inquiry. Christ is on the Cross when darkness begins, and His death follows later. I have approached that darkness as a point at which the ordinary view from below is plainly insufficient. I leave room for an actual divine action upon time there. A clock can mark our chosen center without containing everything that may be happening at it.6

The comparisons developed through the book have given this proposal definite forms. The half-week, the calendar of offerings, and the courses of Moses and Joshua keep their own settings. Their relationship to Christ is what I have been pursuing. I am suggesting that fulfillment may be the reason an earlier pattern has the form it does.

The long civil-day count belongs here too. In the received framework we adopted, 77 × 153 = 11,781 complete days fill the midnight window from January 1, AD 1 opening through all of Crucifixion Friday, closing at April 4, AD 33 opening. That is the frame of the authorized chart. Its exactness belongs to those stated boundaries; the hour of the childhood ceremony is not reported.7

I have read that count beside forgiveness, the offerings, and the fish brought to shore. The altar's ledger and Isaac's covenant sign have also been considered in their own settings. These comparisons have brought us repeatedly to the body given, the living Temple, and the people whose lives are bound to Him. The numbers matter because of what they may be saying about Christ.

John gives us room to think this way. The disciples remember and understand the saying about His body after He has risen. A word already spoken becomes clearer through an event already accomplished. I am extending that direction of thought when I ask whether fulfillment can give earlier history its order as well as disclose its meaning. The further proposal is mine; it is the possibility this book has been asking the reader to consider.8

Heaven has its own witnesses

The heavenly perspective is vital to this argument. If we restrict the event to what someone in Jerusalem could have seen, we have already decided how large its audience is allowed to be. I see no reason to make that decision in advance. The Cross concerns the creation, including the part of it that does not report to us.

The visible sky is itself created. It is not the whole of heaven. But it can carry signs of an action whose reach includes the unseen world. I regard that heavenly reality as at least as important as the earthly scene, and more enduring. It belongs in the account from the beginning.

The partial eclipse at the edge of local visibility is a case in point. Whether people around Jerusalem noticed its ending phase does not settle whether God intended it as a witness. The threshold of sunset and moonrise may be part of the meaning. A sign can stand at the limit of human sight and still have an audience.

The daylight procession raises the same question. Its bodies were present whether the people below could see them or understand their arrangement. I suggest that the heavenly host could have understood a testimony that passed unnoticed on earth. Human recognition need not be the condition upon which divine intention depends.9

This is consistent with the way I am approaching John's writings. His Gospel begins with the Word before it brings us into the human encounters. Revelation opens the heavenly scene and shows earthly conflict within a larger conflict. The Lamb's death belongs to the life and judgment of the whole creation. The heavenly response is part of the event's meaning.

Paul likewise speaks of God's wisdom being made known to rulers and authorities in the heavenly realms through the church. The witnesses on earth are being watched from elsewhere. We cannot assume that a narrative's human audience is its only audience, or that what we fail to see has therefore failed to speak.10

That is why I have asked the reader to come upward with John. I am trying to read the events of earth from the heavenly side as well as from our side. It changes what we consider relevant. Concealment, delayed recognition, and a sign seen by no human observer can then have a place in the account.

This does not require me to put a private calculation into John's mind. I am reading his testimony as inspired and asking whether it carries relationships beyond the understanding it reports. Nor do I need every participant to have understood the calendar arrangement at the time. The extent of God's work is not set by the extent of their awareness.

Carrying the view into Book 2

Book 1 has concentrated on the Passion. Book 2 returns to the beginning of Christ's earthly life and looks more closely at the heavens around His birth. We will then follow the proposed signs across the longer history, from Abraham through the Exodus and Conquest to the first-century events, Jerusalem's fall, and the Gospel spreading among the nations around AD 70.

The birth is the proper place to resume. The One whose body is given at the Cross first enters the world in a body. John's Word becomes flesh. Revelation places the birth and the child's reception at God's throne within a scene that includes heaven, earth, and their conflict. The earthly arrival and the heavenly significance belong to one story.11

Book 2 will examine solar and lunar signs, planetary movements, and eclipse paths beside Scripture's account of Christ and His people. The comparisons reach back through Israel's earlier history and outward into the world through which the Gospel spread. The concern is how the created heavens may bear witness to what God is accomplishing on earth.

I intend to keep both parts of that inquiry in view. Dates and positions have to be stated correctly. The meaning is considered through Scripture. Establishing where a body stood is one task; asking what its place may signify is another. Neither task is helped by confusing it with the other.

The larger perspective also matters when the story reaches Jerusalem. The fall of a city and the loss of its Temple are earthly events with human suffering that must be taken seriously. The Gospel's spread carries the account beyond the city. Revelation asks us to see earthly power, judgment, and the witness of Christ's people in relation to heaven. That is the view from which I want to examine the history that follows.

Abraham, Israel, Christ, Jerusalem, and the nations belong within the history we will be following. The different periods will retain their own events. The proposal is that they participate in one purpose whose center is Christ. Book 2 takes that same question into the sky and across the larger span.

Where this leaves the reader

I have not written this book simply to secure agreement on a Passion chronology. I want the reader to see why the apparent divisions may belong within a much larger act of God. The Cross may gather more than our calendars can display, and its reach may be more actual than our usual account of time permits us to imagine.

I am not embarrassed to say that I think God may have done this deliberately. He can order an event for people who will understand it later, for heavenly beings who understand it differently, or for both. We are not entitled to make our own recognition the measure of His purpose.

John's final city gives the heavenly direction of the inquiry its fullest setting. God and the Lamb supply its light. The same vision retains fruit for each month, service, and continuing reign. I leave those features together. Time's fulfillment can be contemplated through them without making the vision say that every form of ordered life has ceased.12

For now, our days continue, and we continue to read from within them. I believe we understand them better when we allow the heavenly perspective its full weight. What we see from below is real. It is also partial. Heaven is part of the reality we are trying to understand, and its life does not depend on our ability to observe it.

The Cross remains the center from which I am looking. In Book 2 we turn back toward Christ's coming and then across the history of Israel and the nations, looking at the heavens as well as the earth. I want to take the reader high enough to see that the earthly story has always had a heavenly side.
